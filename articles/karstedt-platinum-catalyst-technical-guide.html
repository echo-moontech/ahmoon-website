Karstedt Catalyst (CAS 68478-92-2): The Platinum Hydrosilylation Catalyst Behind Addition-Cure Silicones

Platinum(0)-1,3-divinyl-1,1,3,3-tetramethyldisiloxane — the industry-standard catalyst that turns vinyl + Si–H into cross-linked silicone with zero by-products. Activity, inhibition, poisoning, Pt concentration selection and dosing, explained.

📅 October 29, 2026 📂 LSR · RTV-2 · HTV · Potting & Coating ⏱️ 9 min read

What Karstedt Catalyst Is

Karstedt's catalyst is platinum(0)-1,3-divinyl-1,1,3,3-tetramethyldisiloxane (CAS 68478-92-2, EC 270-844-4, molecular weight 381.48 g/mol) — a Pt(0) complex in which the platinum atom is chelated by the two vinyl groups of a divinyltetramethyldisiloxane ligand. First described by Bruce Karstedt at General Electric in the 1970s, it has since become the default catalyst for silicone hydrosilylation worldwide.

Commercially, it is not sold as the bare complex but as a solution in vinyl-terminated polydimethylsiloxane (VT-PDMS), at a defined platinum concentration — typically 500 to 10,000 ppm Pt for ready-to-use grades, and up to 200,000 ppm for concentrate used in compounding. The vinyl-functional carrier is fully compatible with silicone formulations and keeps the catalyst stable and homogeneous.

Why platinum? Because no other industrial catalyst can match its selectivity and activity for the reaction that defines addition-cure silicones: hydrosilylation — the addition of a Si–H bond across a carbon–carbon double bond to form a stable Si–C bond.

How It Works: The Hydrosilylation Reaction

Addition-cure silicone chemistry combines two components:

  • a vinyl-functional siloxane (vinyl silicone oil, vinyl rubber, LSR base polymer), and
  • a hydrogen-functional crosslinker (hydrogen silicone oil, Si–H).

Neither reacts on its own at room temperature in any useful timeframe. The Karstedt complex activates the Si–H bond through the classical Chalk–Harrod mechanism: oxidative addition of Si–H to Pt(0), coordination and insertion of the vinyl group, then reductive elimination to form the new Si–C bond. The net reaction is:

Si–H + CH₂=CH–Si ⇒ Si–CH₂–CH₂–Si  (no by-products)

Three consequences follow, and they are the reason addition-cure silicones dominate precision applications:

  1. Zero by-products — unlike condensation cure (which releases alcohol or acetic acid), nothing volatilizes off. Shrinkage is minimal, and thick sections cure through without bubbles.
  2. Depth-independent cure — the reaction is chemical, not moisture-driven, so 50 mm castings cure as fast at the core as at the surface.
  3. Heat-accelerated — with the right inhibitor package, the mix is stable for hours at 25 °C yet gels in minutes at 100–120 °C. That is the whole basis of LSR injection molding.

Activity: How Cure Speed Is Measured

Two numbers from our specification describe real production behavior:

  • Room-temperature activity (25 °C thickening time): ≤ 10 minutes. After the catalyst and inhibitor are balanced, a catalyzed mix begins to thicken within this window — long enough to process, short enough to know the catalyst is alive.
  • High-temperature activity (120 °C × 25 min): entirely cured. A standard test coupon cures completely, confirming full crosslinking capability.

Formulators more commonly track T10 activity — the time for viscosity to start rising in a defined test. A fresh batch of our PT5000 grade recently tested at T10 = 81 s against a ≤ 100 s specification (batch COA, May 2024), comfortably inside spec. Choosing the Right Pt Concentration & Dosing

Catalyst level is almost always quoted as ppm of platinum metal relative to the total formulation — not as ppm of the catalyst solution. Converting is simple arithmetic:

  1. Read the Pt concentration off the grade: 8000 ppm = 0.8% Pt by weight.
  2. Dose by phr (parts per hundred rubber). Adding 1 phr of an 8000 ppm catalyst to a 100-part formulation contributes 0.8 ÷ 101 × 100 ≈ 79 ppm Pt in the cured mix.
  3. Adjust upward for faster cure or thicker sections; adjust downward when inhibition risk is high or optical clarity matters.

Which grade to stock? A practical rule: choose one concentration and vary the phr, rather than stocking many concentrations. 5000–8000 ppm grades give comfortable dosing accuracy for most LSR, RTV-2 and potting work; 10000+ ppm suits high-speed molding; lower grades (500–2000 ppm) are easier to meter in sensitive optical or coating systems. Every concentration is prepared from the same certified Pt(0) complex, so activity is consistent across the menu.

Cure Inhibition: The Poisons That Kill Platinum

Karstedt catalyst is extraordinarily sensitive to trace contaminants. A batch that cures slowly, shows tacky surfaces or never gels is almost always poisoned, not under-dosed. The usual suspects:

  • Amines — amine-containing additives, some epoxy hardeners, amine odors from nearby processes
  • Organotin compounds — the dibutyltin / dioctyltin catalysts used in condensation-cure RTV-1; never share tooling between the two cure systems
  • Sulfur & polysulfides — sulfur-vulcanized rubber tooling, some gaskets, latex gloves
  • Phosphorus compounds — certain flame retardants and plasticizers
  • Unverified fillers — precipitated silica or pigments with residual amine or sulfur surface treatment

Prevention checklist: dedicated mixing equipment for addition-cure lines; platinum-safe fillers with COA; nitrile (not latex) gloves; separate curing ovens from condensation-cure production; and when in doubt, run a small inhibition test before committing a production batch. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is Karstedt catalyst?
Karstedt catalyst is platinum(0)-1,3-divinyl-1,1,3,3-tetramethyldisiloxane (CAS 68478-92-2), a Pt(0) complex in a vinyl-terminated siloxane carrier. It is the industry-standard catalyst for hydrosilylation (addition-cure) of silicones, catalyzing Si–H addition across vinyl groups with no by-products. It is used in LSR, RTV-2, HTV molding and extrusion, potting compounds, silicone coatings and release coatings.
Q: How much platinum catalyst should I add?
Catalyst level is quoted as ppm of platinum metal relative to the total formulation; typical hydrosilylation systems run in the single-digit to tens-of-ppm Pt range with an inhibitor balancing pot life and cure speed. Worked example: 1 phr of an 8000 ppm (0.8% Pt) catalyst in a 100-part formulation ≈ 79 ppm Pt. We help you select concentration and dosage for your system.
Q: Why did my catalyst stop working (no cure)?
Platinum is poisoned by traces of amines, organotin (condensation-cure catalysts), sulfur and polysulfides, phosphorus compounds and contaminated fillers — symptoms are delayed cure or tacky surfaces. Use clean dedicated tooling, segregate addition-cure from condensation-cure lines, verify fillers are platinum-safe, and run an inhibition test when in doubt.
Q: What Pt concentrations are available?
Standard grades: 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 5000, 6000, 8000 and 10000 ppm Pt in vinyl-terminated PDMS; any content up to 200000 ppm concentrate can be customized. All grades derive from the same certified Pt(0) complex (assay ≥20%), so activity is consistent across concentrations.
Q: How should Karstedt catalyst be stored?
Sealed in the dark at 20–30°C for up to 12 months — activity and appearance stay unchanged. Keep away from sulfur, phosphorus, amine and organotin contamination. Flash point is around 30°C, so store as a flammable liquid away from ignition sources. Cold-climate shipments include heat packs.
